# Latin Terms & Maxims

219 entries headwords only

## a fortiori

From the stronger argument.

Pronunciation ay for-shee-OR-eye **Plain language:** If a fact is true, then a less controversial fact must also be true. **Example:** If the law prohibits minor theft, then a fortiori it prohibits major larceny.

## a posteriori

This describes knowledge or justification that is dependent on empirical experience or evidence. In law, it refers to conclusions drawn from observing actual facts rather than theoretical deduction.

Plain language Knowledge gained from experience or observation.

## a priori

From the former.

Pronunciation ay pree-OR-eye **Plain language:** Reasoning based on theoretical deduction rather than empirical observation. **Example:** The judge rejected the argument because it was an a priori assumption not supported by facts.

## ab initio

From the beginning.

Pronunciation ab ih-NISH-ee-oh **Plain language:** Something that is considered invalid from the very moment it started. **Example:** The contract was declared void ab initio because one party lacked legal capacity. **Category:** contract

## acta non verba

This phrase translates to deeds, not words. It suggests that actions are more significant than spoken promises or intentions in legal and personal contexts.

Plain language Actions speak louder than words.

## Actio personalis moritur cum persona

This maxim holds that a personal cause of action does not survive the death of either the plaintiff or the defendant. In modern law, this has been largely modified by survival statutes.

Category civil

## Actus reus

The guilty act; the physical or external element of a crime, consisting of a voluntary act or omission that causes the prohibited harm.

Pronunciation AK-tus RAY-us **Plain language:** The actual wrongful deed that makes up a crime -- you have to have done something, not just thought about it. **Example:** To convict, the prosecution must prove both the mens rea (guilty mind) and the actus reus (guilty act).

## ad damnum

To the damage.

Pronunciation ad DAM-num **Plain language:** The section of a complaint that specifies the amount of money sought. **Example:** The plaintiff increased the ad damnum clause in the amended complaint. **Category:** procedural

## ad hoc

For this purpose.

Pronunciation ad HOCK **Plain language:** Created or done for a specific situation rather than as part of a general plan. **Example:** An ad hoc committee was formed to investigate the procedural error.

## ad hominem

A fallacious argument that attacks the person making the claim rather than addressing the substance of the argument itself. It is often used to discredit a witness or opponent by focusing on their character instead of evidence.

Plain language Attacking the person instead of the argument. **Category:** procedural

## ad idem

This refers to a meeting of the minds between two or more parties entering into a contract. It signifies that all parties fully understand and agree to the same essential terms.

Plain language Everyone is on the same page. **Category:** contract

## Ad infinitum

Used to describe a process that continues forever without end. In legal arguments, it refers to an interminable series of events or claims.

## ad litem

For the suit.

Pronunciation ad LY-tem **Plain language:** Appointed for the purposes of a specific lawsuit. **Example:** The court appointed a guardian ad litem to protect the child's interests. **Category:** procedural

## ad nauseam

Refers to an argument or subject that has been repeated so frequently that it becomes tiresome or annoying. In legal settings, it can describe repetitive motions that do not contribute new information.

Plain language Discussing something until it is sick or repetitive.

## affidavit

He has pledged.

Pronunciation af-ih-DAY-vit **Plain language:** A written statement confirmed by oath or affirmation. **Example:** The witness signed an affidavit regarding the accident. **Category:** evidence

## alieni juris

Of another's law.

Pronunciation ay-lee-EN-ee JOO-ris **Plain language:** Someone subject to the legal authority of another, like a minor. **Example:** The child was considered alieni juris. **Category:** family

## Aliunde

Refers to evidence or information provided from a source other than the document or record under consideration. It is often used in the context of interpreting contracts or wills.

## amici curiae

Refers to a friend of the court, typically a person or organization that is not a party to a lawsuit but assists the court by offering information or insight. Their input is usually provided through a brief regarding a matter of law or fact.

Plain language An outside expert providing helpful information to the judge. **Category:** procedural

## Amicus curiae

Friend of the court; a non-party who submits information or argument to assist the court in deciding a case.

Pronunciation uh-MEE-kus KYOO-ree-ee **Plain language:** An outside expert or group giving the court extra input on an important case. **Example:** The ACLU filed an amicus curiae brief supporting the plaintiff.

## animus

This term refers to the intention, mind, or purpose behind an act. It is often crucial in determining criminal intent or the intent required for a valid transfer of property.

Plain language The mental intention behind an action.

## animus furandi

Intention of stealing.

Pronunciation AN-ih-mus fur-AN-dee **Plain language:** The intent to commit theft. **Example:** The defendant acted with animus furandi when he entered the store. **Category:** criminal

## Animus revertendi

Used in property law regarding animals or persons to determine if something remains under the control of the owner. It signifies the intent to come back to a specific place.

Category property

## ante

Before.

Pronunciation AN-tee **Plain language:** Occurring or situated before. **Example:** The contract was signed ante the start of the project.

## arguendo

In the course of arguing.

Pronunciation ar-gyoo-EN-doh **Plain language:** Assuming something for the sake of argument. **Example:** Arguendo, even if the defendant were negligent, there were no resulting damages. **Category:** procedural

## bona fide

In good faith.

Pronunciation BOH-nuh FY-dee **Plain language:** Genuine, real, or without intent to deceive. **Example:** The buyer was a bona fide purchaser for value without notice of the lien. **Category:** contract

## Bona fides

Refers to the state of mind of being honest and sincere in a transaction or action. It is a critical requirement in contract negotiations and business dealings.

Category contract

## bona vacantia

Vacant goods.

Pronunciation BOH-nuh vuh-KAN-shah **Plain language:** Property that has no owner and goes to the state. **Example:** The unclaimed estate was declared bona vacantia. **Category:** property

## cadit quaestio

The question falls.

Pronunciation KAY-dit KWES-tee-oh **Plain language:** The matter is settled or no longer an issue. **Example:** Once the law was repealed, the legal dispute cadit quaestio. **Category:** procedural

## casus belli

Occasion for war.

Pronunciation KAY-sus BEL-eye **Plain language:** An act that provokes or justifies war. **Example:** The border violation served as the casus belli. **Category:** constitutional

## caveat

Let him beware.

Pronunciation KAV-ee-at **Plain language:** A formal warning or qualification. **Example:** He signed the agreement with a caveat regarding future costs.

## Caveat emptor

Let the buyer beware; the principle that a buyer purchases at their own risk in the absence of an express warranty or fraud.

Pronunciation KAY-vee-ot EMP-tor **Plain language:** Buy at your own risk -- inspect before you pay. **Example:** For used goods without warranty, caveat emptor applies.

## caveat venditor

Let the seller beware.

Pronunciation KAV-ee-at ven-DEE-tor **Plain language:** The principle that the seller is responsible for the quality of goods. **Example:** Under the new consumer laws, the principle of caveat venditor applies to retailers. **Category:** contract

## Certiorari

A writ issued by a higher court ordering a lower court to send up the record for review; the mechanism by which the U.S. Supreme Court selects cases.

Pronunciation ser-shee-oh-RAR-ee **Plain language:** A formal request for a higher court to look at a lower court's decision. **Example:** The Supreme Court granted certiorari to review the constitutional challenge.

## Cessante ratione legis cessat ipsa lex

A principle meaning that when the underlying logic or purpose of a legal rule no longer exists, the rule itself should no longer be applied. It is a tool for judicial interpretation.

## ceteris paribus

This phrase means with all other things being equal. It is often used in legal and economic analysis to predict the outcome of a scenario by holding external variables constant.

Plain language Assuming all other factors remain the same.

## circa

Around or about.

Pronunciation SIR-kuh **Plain language:** Used to indicate an approximate date. **Example:** The document was dated circa 1920.

## Communis error facit jus

Suggests that if an error is so widespread and accepted that it becomes the common practice, it may eventually be recognized as the legal standard. It serves as a justification for established customs.

## compos mentis

Master of one's mind.

Pronunciation KOM-pos MEN-tis **Plain language:** Mentally competent. **Example:** The testator was compos mentis when the will was drafted. **Category:** civil

## consortium

Fellowship.

Pronunciation con-SOR-shee-um **Plain language:** The legal right to the company, society, and assistance of a spouse. **Example:** The spouse sued for loss of consortium. **Category:** tort

## contra

Against.

Pronunciation KON-truh **Plain language:** Used to cite a source that opposes the proposition being made. **Example:** See Smith v. Jones, 100 U.S. 20 (2000); contra Doe v. Roe, 200 U.S. 50 (2005). **Category:** procedural

## coram nobis

A legal writ that allows a court to correct its own judgment if a fundamental error of fact is discovered that was not known at the time of the initial trial. It is a procedural tool used to seek justice after a verdict has been rendered.

Plain language A process to fix a factual error in a court case. **Category:** procedural

## Coram non judice

Describes a legal proceeding conducted by a court or judge who lacks the proper jurisdiction. A judgment rendered in such a case is considered void.

Category procedural

## corpus

Refers to the body or the physical substance of something, such as the body of a crime or the capital assets of a trust. In criminal law, it is essential for proving that an offense actually occurred.

Plain language The physical body or main asset.

## Corpus delicti

The body of the crime; the objective proof that a crime actually occurred, traditionally including both the harm and its criminal cause.

Pronunciation KOR-pus de-LIK-tie **Plain language:** Evidence that a crime truly happened -- not just that the accused confessed. **Example:** A confession alone cannot convict without independent proof of the corpus delicti.

## corpus juris

Body of law.

Pronunciation KOR-pus JOO-ris **Plain language:** The entire collection of laws in a jurisdiction. **Example:** The judge reviewed the corpus juris regarding tax liability.

## cui bono

To whose advantage?

Pronunciation kyoo BOH-no **Plain language:** Asking who benefits from a particular event or crime. **Example:** In searching for the culprit, the police asked cui bono. **Category:** criminal

## Culpa lata

Refers to a level of negligence that is extreme or reckless, showing a total disregard for the safety or rights of others. It goes beyond simple carelessness.

Category civil

## damnum absque injuria

Damage without legal injury.

Pronunciation DAM-num ABS-kway in-JOOR-ee-uh **Plain language:** Suffering a loss but having no legal right to sue. **Example:** The business loss was damnum absque injuria. **Category:** tort

## Damnum fatale

A loss caused by an inevitable accident or act of God that could not have been prevented by reasonable human foresight. It is often used as a defense against liability.

Category civil

## de bonis non

This refers to the goods of a deceased person that have not yet been administered by an executor. It typically occurs when a primary executor dies or is removed, requiring a successor to manage the remaining estate.

Plain language Leftover property of an estate that still needs to be handled. **Category:** civil

## de facto

In fact.

Pronunciation dee FAK-toh **Plain language:** Something that exists in practice, though not officially recognized. **Example:** They were the de facto leaders of the organization.

## de jure

Of right.

Pronunciation dee JOOR-ee **Plain language:** According to the law; official. **Example:** The merger was de jure complete upon filing.

## de minimis

Concerning the smallest things.

Pronunciation dee MIN-ih-miss **Plain language:** The law does not concern itself with trifles or insignificant matters. **Example:** The court dismissed the claim as de minimis.

## de novo

Anew.

Pronunciation dee NO-voh **Plain language:** Starting over from the beginning, as if the first trial never happened. **Example:** The appellate court reviewed the administrative decision de novo. **Category:** procedural

## Delegatus non potest delegare

The principle that an individual to whom authority has been delegated cannot further delegate that authority to another person. It is commonly applied in agency law.

Category contract

## dies non

Refers to a day on which no legal business can be transacted, such as a Sunday or a public holiday. On these days, the courts are closed and legal deadlines are generally suspended.

Plain language A day where the court is closed for business. **Category:** procedural

## doli incapax

Incapable of malice.

Pronunciation DOH-lee in-KAY-paks **Plain language:** The legal principle that young children cannot be held criminally responsible. **Example:** The child was doli incapax under the local statutes. **Category:** criminal

## donatio mortis causa

Gift in anticipation of death.

Pronunciation doh-NAY-shee-oh MOR-tis KAW-zuh **Plain language:** A gift given by someone who expects to die soon. **Example:** The court analyzed the donatio mortis causa for validity. **Category:** property

## duces tecum

Bring with you.

Pronunciation DOO-sis TEE-kum **Plain language:** A legal order to bring specific items to court. **Example:** The officer served a duces tecum order. **Category:** evidence

## Ejusdem generis

Of the same kind; the canon that a general catch-all phrase following specific terms is limited to items of the same class as those listed.

Pronunciation ee-yus-dem JEN-er-is **Plain language:** A general phrase at the end of a list only covers stuff like the listed examples. **Example:** A statute listing 'cars, trucks, and other vehicles' applies ejusdem generis only to road vehicles.

## eo nomine

Meaning by that name, this term is used to indicate that something is referred to specifically or by its precise designation. It is often used in statutes to define exactly what is covered under a law.

Plain language Under that specific name or title.

## et al.

And others.

Pronunciation et AL **Plain language:** Used in lists of names to indicate other parties are involved. **Example:** The case is titled Jones v. Smith et al. **Category:** procedural

## et seq.

And the following.

Pronunciation et SEEK-wee **Plain language:** Refers to the sections or pages immediately following the one cited. **Example:** Refer to the code section 10.1 et seq. **Category:** procedural

## et ux

And wife.

Pronunciation et UKS **Plain language:** Indicates a couple is named in a legal document. **Example:** The deed was signed by John Doe et ux. **Category:** property

## ex cathedra

Literally meaning from the chair, it refers to an authoritative pronouncement made by someone in a position of power. While often theological, in a legal sense, it implies a decision made with the full authority of the office.

Plain language A statement made with official authority.

## Ex dolo malo non oritur actio

A legal principle that prevents a plaintiff from bringing a claim if the cause of action is rooted in their own illegal or fraudulent conduct. It acts as an equitable bar.

Category civil

## ex gratia

Out of favor.

Pronunciation ex GRAY-shee-uh **Plain language:** Done as a voluntary favor rather than a legal requirement. **Example:** The payment was made ex gratia to avoid litigation. **Category:** civil

## Ex parte

On one side only; a proceeding or order made for the benefit of one party without notice to the other, permitted in limited emergency situations.

Pronunciation eks PAR-tay **Plain language:** A court action done for one side without telling the other, usually for emergencies. **Example:** The plaintiff obtained an ex parte restraining order before the defendant was notified.

## Ex post

Refers to an analysis or action taken after an event has already occurred. In law, it is often used to evaluate the reasonableness of past actions.

## ex post facto

After the fact.

Pronunciation ex post FAK-toh **Plain language:** A law that retroactively changes the consequences of actions that were committed before the enactment of the law. **Example:** The Constitution prohibits ex post facto laws in criminal cases. **Category:** constitutional

## ex rel

On the relation of.

Pronunciation ex REL **Plain language:** Legal action brought by the government on behalf of a private individual. **Example:** The state filed the suit ex rel the Attorney General. **Category:** procedural

## ex turpi causa

A legal doctrine stating that a plaintiff cannot pursue a legal remedy if their claim arises in connection with their own illegal act. Courts will not assist a party who bases their cause of action upon an immoral or illegal foundation.

Plain language You cannot sue someone for damages if you were injured while committing an illegal act yourself. **Category:** tort

## expressio unius

A principle of statutory interpretation meaning that the express mention of one thing excludes all others. If a statute lists specific items, it is presumed that items not listed were intentionally omitted.

Plain language By specifically mentioning one thing, you imply that everything else is left out.

## Expressio unius est exclusio alterius

The expression of one thing is the exclusion of the other; the canon that listing some items implies the exclusion of unlisted ones.

Pronunciation ex-PRES-ee-oh OO-nee-us est eks-KLOO-zee-oh al-TER-ee-us **Plain language:** If a law lists only certain things, it deliberately leaves out the rest. **Example:** Mentioning 'dogs and cats' by expressio unius excludes birds.

## facsimile

Make similar.

Pronunciation fak-SIM-ih-lee **Plain language:** An exact copy of a document. **Example:** A facsimile of the contract was sent to the attorney.

## felo de se

A historical legal term for a person who takes their own life. It was historically treated as a crime, though it is now largely obsolete in modern jurisprudence.

Plain language A historical term referring to someone who has committed suicide. **Category:** criminal

## ferae naturae

Refers to wild animals that are not under the control of humans and are not domesticated. Property rights over these animals are generally acquired only by capturing or killing them.

Plain language Refers to wild animals that are not owned by anyone until they are caught. **Category:** property

## fiat

Let it be done.

Pronunciation FY-at **Plain language:** An official order or decree. **Example:** The executive issued a fiat to authorize the funds. **Category:** constitutional

## fiat justitia

Part of a larger phrase meaning 'let justice be done, though the heavens may fall.' It represents the principle that justice must be administered regardless of the consequences.

Plain language Justice must be carried out even if the results are difficult or disruptive.

## fiducia

An ancient form of contract where property was transferred to another person under a trust or agreement that it would be returned under specific conditions. It serves as an early predecessor to modern trust and security arrangements.

Plain language A contract where you give property to someone else on the condition that they hold it in trust for you. **Category:** contract

## fieri facias

Cause to be made.

Pronunciation FY-er-ee FAY-shee-us **Plain language:** A court order to seize a debtor's property to satisfy a judgment. **Example:** The sheriff executed the writ of fieri facias. **Category:** procedural

## flagrante delicto

A term describing a person caught in the act of committing a crime. It is often used to justify immediate arrest without a warrant.

Plain language Caught red-handed while in the middle of a crime. **Category:** criminal

## Forum non conveniens

An inconvenient forum; the doctrine allowing a court to dismiss a case when another court is more appropriate and available to the parties.

Pronunciation FOR-um non kon-vee-EN-see-enz **Plain language:** A court can send a case to a better-fitting court. **Example:** The judge dismissed under forum non conveniens because the witnesses and evidence were in another state.

## functus officio

Having performed his office.

Pronunciation FUNK-tus oh-FISH-ee-oh **Plain language:** An official who has completed their task and no longer has authority. **Example:** The arbitrator was functus officio once the award was issued. **Category:** procedural

## guardian ad litem

A guardian for the lawsuit.

Pronunciation GAR-dee-un ad LY-tem **Plain language:** A person appointed by the court to represent the interests of a minor or incapacitated person during legal proceedings. **Example:** The judge appointed a guardian ad litem to represent the child in the divorce. **Category:** family

## Habeas corpus

You have the body; a writ challenging the lawfulness of a person's detention, requiring the government to justify holding someone.

Pronunciation HAY-bee-us KOR-pus **Plain language:** A legal order that forces the government to explain why it is keeping someone locked up. **Example:** The inmate filed a writ of habeas corpus challenging his conviction as unconstitutional.

## habeas data

Have the data.

Pronunciation HAY-bee-us DAY-tuh **Plain language:** The right of individuals to know what information agencies have about them. **Example:** The citizen filed for habeas data to check his records. **Category:** constitutional

## ibid.

In the same place.

Pronunciation IB-id **Plain language:** Used in citations to refer to the same work or source. **Example:** Ibid, at page 12. **Category:** procedural

## id.

The same.

Pronunciation ID **Plain language:** Used in citations to refer to the immediately preceding authority. **Example:** Id. at 45. **Category:** procedural

## Ignorantia juris non excusat

A foundational principle stating that a person who violates the law cannot use the defense that they were unaware of the legal prohibition. It assumes all citizens have a duty to know the law.

Category criminal

## in absentia

Refers to a legal proceeding conducted while the defendant is not physically present. This often occurs when a defendant absconds or refuses to appear in court.

Plain language The legal process happens while the accused person is not present in court. **Category:** procedural

## in camera

In a chamber.

Pronunciation in KAM-uh-ruh **Plain language:** Proceedings held in private, usually in the judge's chambers. **Example:** The judge reviewed the confidential documents in camera. **Category:** procedural

## in extremis

Describes a person who is at the point of death or in the final stages of life. Statements or actions made in this state may carry special legal weight, such as in the context of dying declarations.

Plain language Being in the final moments of life.

## in flagrante

Similar to flagrante delicto, it means being caught in the middle of an act. It is frequently used in common parlance to describe catching someone in the act of infidelity or another private offense.

Plain language Being discovered right while doing something wrong.

## in flagrante delicto

In blazing offense.

Pronunciation in fluh-GRAN-tee deh-LIK-toh **Plain language:** Caught in the act of committing a crime. **Example:** The police caught the thief in flagrante delicto. **Category:** criminal

## In forma pauperis

In the manner of a pauper; a status allowing an indigent litigant to proceed without paying filing fees.

Pronunciation in FOR-muh PAW-puh-ris **Plain language:** A way to sue without paying court fees because you're too poor to afford them. **Example:** The court granted her motion to proceed in forma pauperis.

## in loco parentis

Refers to a person or entity that assumes the parental responsibilities, care, and control of a child without a formal adoption. It is commonly applied to schools and guardians authorized to act on behalf of a minor.

Plain language Acting in the role and responsibility of a parent. **Category:** family

## in pari delicto

In equal fault.

Pronunciation in PARE-ee deh-LIK-toh **Plain language:** A legal doctrine where both parties are equally at fault, preventing one from suing the other. **Example:** The court dismissed the claim because both parties were in pari delicto. **Category:** civil

## In pari materia

A rule of statutory construction where statutes dealing with the same subject matter are interpreted together. This helps ensure consistency across related legislation.

## In personam

Against the person; a judgment imposing personal liability on a specific individual or entity, binding only those parties.

Pronunciation in per-SOH-nam **Plain language:** A case against a person personally, to make them pay or perform. **Example:** A money judgment is in personam, enforceable against the debtor's assets.

## In rem

Against the thing; a proceeding directed at property itself to determine its status or title, with jurisdiction based on the property's location.

Pronunciation in REM **Plain language:** A case about a thing (property), not about a person's personal obligations. **Example:** A foreclosure is an in rem action against the mortgaged property.

## in situ

In its place.

Pronunciation in SIT-yoo **Plain language:** In the original or existing position. **Example:** The evidence was analyzed in situ. **Category:** evidence

## in terrorem

This refers to a clause or provision intended to frighten or intimidate persons into performing an action or abstaining from one. It is most commonly seen in wills where a clause threatens to disinherit any beneficiary who challenges the validity of the document.

Plain language A legal threat used to discourage someone from taking a specific action. **Category:** property

## in toto

In total.

Pronunciation in TOH-toh **Plain language:** Completely or in its entirety. **Example:** The court rejected the defense's argument in toto. **Category:** procedural

## In transitu

Refers to goods that are in the process of being shipped or moved from one location to another. This status is significant for determining the transfer of title or risk of loss.

Category property

## infra

Below.

Pronunciation IN-fruh **Plain language:** Used to refer to material that appears later in the same document. **Example:** See the discussion infra at section IV. **Category:** procedural

## infra dignitatem

Below dignity.

Pronunciation IN-fruh dig-nih-TAH-tem **Plain language:** Beneath the dignity of the court. **Example:** The judge found the petty quarrel infra dignitatem. **Category:** procedural

## Injuria absque damno

A situation where a legal right has been violated but no actual loss or financial harm resulted. It typically does not allow for the recovery of damages.

Category civil

## inter alia

Among other things.

Pronunciation in-ter AL-ee-uh **Plain language:** Used when only some of the relevant items are listed. **Example:** The contract includes, inter alia, clauses regarding confidentiality and liability. **Category:** contract

## inter se

A Latin phrase meaning between or among themselves. It is used to describe rights or obligations that exist between parties independently of their relationship with third parties.

Plain language Between the parties themselves.

## Ipso facto

By the fact itself; a consequence that follows automatically from the act or status itself rather than from a separate legal rule.

Pronunciation IP-soh FAK-toh **Plain language:** By the very fact of it -- automatic result. **Example:** Refusing to deliver the goods makes the breach ipso facto.

## ipso jure

By the law itself.

Pronunciation IP-soh JOO-ree **Plain language:** A consequence that happens automatically by operation of law. **Example:** His status as a citizen was granted ipso jure. **Category:** constitutional

## iter criminis

This represents the path or progression of a crime from the initial thought to the final consummation. It maps the stages from intention and preparation to the attempt and eventual execution of the offense.

Plain language The step-by-step process of committing a crime. **Category:** criminal

## Jura novit curia

The principle that the judge is responsible for knowing and applying the correct law to the facts of a case, even if the attorneys fail to cite it. The parties are only required to provide the facts.

Category procedural

## jus cogens

This refers to peremptory norms of international law that are accepted and recognized by the international community as a whole. No derogation is permitted from these norms, and they can only be modified by a subsequent norm of general international law having the same character.

Plain language Fundamental principles of international law that cannot be violated under any circumstances.

## jus gentium

Law of nations.

Pronunciation jus JEN-tee-um **Plain language:** Principles of international law. **Example:** The tribunal applied the jus gentium to resolve the dispute.

## jus naturale

This refers to natural law, which is a system of law based on a close observation of human nature and based on values intrinsic to human nature. It suggests that laws are inherent in the natural order of the world, rather than solely established by human institutions.

Plain language Laws derived from nature or inherent human morality rather than government decree.

## lacuna

This denotes a gap or deficiency in a legal system where no specific law or regulation applies to a particular situation. It often requires judges to interpret existing laws by analogy or principles to fill the void.

Plain language An empty space or missing rule in the legal system. **Category:** procedural

## lex fori

This means the law of the forum or the law of the place where the lawsuit is filed. It generally dictates the procedural rules of the case, regardless of which jurisdiction's substantive law governs the merits.

Plain language The laws of the country or state where the court case is being held. **Category:** procedural

## lex loci

This refers to the law of the place where a particular event occurred or a transaction was completed. It is frequently applied in conflict of laws to determine which jurisdiction's rules govern the validity of a contract or the nature of a tort.

Plain language The law of the place where the event or transaction happened.

## Lex loci contractus

The law of the place of the contract; the rule that a contract is governed by the law of the jurisdiction where it was made or to be performed, absent a choice-of-law clause.

Pronunciation leks LO-kee kon-TRAK-tus **Plain language:** Contracts follow the law of the place where they were made. **Example:** The court applied Missouri law under lex loci contractus because the contract was signed there.

## Lex loci delicti

A choice-of-law principle where the court applies the law of the jurisdiction where the tort occurred. It is used to resolve conflicts of law in interstate cases.

Category civil

## Lex non cogit ad impossibilia

A maxim stating that the law cannot require individuals to perform acts that are physically or legally impossible. It is used as a defense in contractual or statutory obligations.

## lex non scripta

This term refers to unwritten law, which is primarily composed of common law and customs that have gained legal force through usage over time. It stands in contrast to statutory or codified law, which is explicitly written and enacted by a legislature.

Plain language Law that is based on custom and judicial precedent rather than written statutes.

## lex scripta

Written law.

Pronunciation lex SKRIP-tuh **Plain language:** Statutory law as opposed to common law. **Example:** The judge looked to the lex scripta for guidance.

## Lis alibi pendens

A plea used when the same cause of action between the same parties is currently being litigated in another court. It serves to prevent duplicative litigation.

Category procedural

## lis pendens

Pending suit.

Pronunciation liss PEN-denz **Plain language:** A notice filed to warn that a property is subject to a lawsuit. **Example:** The bank filed a lis pendens on the house. **Category:** property

## Locus delicti

Refers to the specific geographical location where a tort or crime was committed. This is essential for determining jurisdiction and venue.

## locus in quo
